Poem 247

The Definition of Irony

*Published in Harbinger in 2016
Journal / archive ↗
“Jump, jump,” they shouted,
And the shout became a howl.
Like the Romans shouting
A mixed cadence of “Barritus,”
Keeping time to the sun’s passage in the sky
 
The crowd came
To see the splat, to see the goo
Like an eggy breakfast on a Sunday afternoon,
And all these thoughts don’t add up right
But hate flies from lips like a caged bird.
 
There were kids down there, goddammit.
(he didn’t know why people squished that word together as they did --
which seemed like the greatest sacrilege,
a portmanteau to combine “god” and “dammit”
without a break.
Hell, even “death row” has a break in it.)
 
“Goddammit.” He said.
Freefalling ethics with no safety net
(to speak of)
 
For oddity is taboo….
 
So he stood at a height of loneliness from greater depths --
Upon a clock tower as high as the hour.
The kid (a kid in his problems and adult in all else)
Looked down at the -- ever growing.
 
What?
Were they people from this height?
Were they people this far from his plight?
 
He heard these chants before:
 
“Cut it up and down,” they said, “not lengthwise,” in their know-it-all-ism.
(they liked to stop and look at car wrecks)
(but never the car)
 
The tale of the masses is old
Dulled in the tar of history.
 
We can say nothing new about them,
So we say nothing at all.
They wanted him dead.
Didn’t they?
 
 
This man, he stood, this man he would…
This man he jeered, this man he cheered.
 
But all of them wanted him to fall
--or rather they wanted him to jump into his stationary fall.
 
“Why was he here?” was what the nicer ones would ask.
But only in soundbite,
without empathy, but without jeer.
They wondered how many stories
He had underneath him
--That would only support the fall.
(Because only The Fall could come from stories).
 
The crowd below looked up
He above looked down.
 
The chant continued and continued…
With his vertigo thoughts
He thought the thoughts he wasn’t having.
He thought the thoughts his thoughts would think.
“Jump, Jump, Jump!”
It could have been written a thousand times.
 
And on the precipice he thought how ironic would it be
(knowing he would be chided this is not irony)
 
To turn the world upside down:
With the crowd above and him below.
 
So he took out from his jacket
What he called his friend.
And let the lead fall as it would.
 
But!
 
Even if poems are meant to be beautiful.
Why are awful subjects dressed in tuxes?
The tragedian could not really cry
That’s why he wore masks to hide.
 
But those below; they could not hide.
From a bullet like a butterfly.
 
Not only was it the most ironic thing of all,
It was the funniest thing he ever saw.
